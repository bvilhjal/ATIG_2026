Answer. allelign found no positions for TG and DBP, and an allele frequency only for DBP. $h^2$ is the slope times $M/N$, so an N that is 10% too large gives an $h^2$ that is 10% too small. Here it hardly matters: DBP's $h^2$ is 0.137 with N_effective and 0.136 with TotalSampleSize.

Q4. Estimate SNP heritability¶

LDSC models the χ² statistic of SNP $j$ as

$$E[\chi^2_j] = 1 + Na + \frac{N h^2}{M}\,\ell_j, \qquad (1)$$

with LD score $\ell_j$, sample size $N$, number of SNPs $M$ and confounding $a$.

Which trait has the highest heritability (Table 7, Figure 2)? Compare the intercepts with λGC, the median χ² divided by 0.455. Why is TG's intercept below one? Set M = ref.m_snps and rerun both cells: what changes?

Answer. BMI 0.214 (SE 0.008), TG 0.176 (SE 0.022), DBP 0.137 (SE 0.007).

λGC is 2.7 for BMI, but the intercept is 1.159, so most of the inflation is polygenic signal. Only 6% of BMI's excess χ², (1.159 − 1)/(3.817 − 1), and 10% of DBP's is in the intercept, and that is an upper bound on confounding. TG's intercept is 0.955 because GLGC corrected each study for genomic control, which lowers its $h^2$ too. TG also depends on a few loci with very large effects: without the χ² cap (chi2_cap=False) its $h^2$ is 0.29.

With M = ref.m_snps, every $h^2$ is 1.68 times larger, while the intercepts and $r_g$ stay the same. M sets which SNPs $h^2$ refers to; the usual choice is the common SNPs (MAF 5–50%).

Q5. BMI and TG¶

Bivariate LDSC regresses the product of the two z scores on LD score:

$$E[z_{1j} z_{2j}] = \frac{\sqrt{N_1 N_2}\,\rho_g}{M}\,\ell_j + \frac{N_s\,\rho}{\sqrt{N_1 N_2}}, \qquad (2)$$

where $\rho_g$ is the genetic covariance, $N_s$ the number of people in both GWAS and $\rho$ their phenotypic correlation. The genetic correlation is $r_g = \rho_g / \sqrt{h^2_1 h^2_2}$.

Is $r_g$ clearly above zero? What does the intercept tell you? Why is the plain correlation of z scores so much smaller? Where does the line in Figure 3 meet the y-axis?

Answer. Higher BMI goes with higher blood pressure, so a positive $r_g$ would be expected, but BMI–DBP gives −0.059 (SE 0.017). Adjusting DBP for BMI subtracts a multiple of each SNP's BMI effect, which pushes the genetic covariance with BMI down. This is collider bias (Aschard et al. 2015). The intercept, 0.003 (SE 0.014), is close to zero because BMI-adjusted DBP is uncorrelated with BMI in UK Biobank. TG–DBP gives 0.095 (SE 0.027).

Q7. Conclude¶

Write three sentences: the BMI–TG $r_g$ with its standard error, why it does not show that BMI raises TG, and what the BMI–DBP result teaches you about published GWAS.

Answer. BMI and TG have a genetic correlation of 0.28 (SE 0.03). It could come from BMI affecting TG, the reverse, pleiotropy or a shared cause, and MR or other designs are needed to tell these apart. Before using a published GWAS, check what it was adjusted for.

Data and methods¶

BMI (GCST006900), DBP (GCST006630) and TG (GLGC) are European-ancestry meta-analyses on GRCh37; see data/sources.json. The TG and DBP results were corrected for inflation before release. The LD scores are the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European scores released with LDSC (Zenodo 7768714). PLDSC fits equations (1) and (2) in one step and drops SNPs with χ² above max(80, N/1000). The original LDSC software uses a two-step estimator, so its estimates differ a little, most for TG. Background: Bulik-Sullivan et al. 2015a and 2015b.
