def manhattan(ax, table):
    """Plot the 1% of SNPs with the smallest P values along the genome."""
    top = table.nsmallest(len(table) // 100, 'p')
    x = top['chrom'] + top['pos'] / 250e6        # Chromosomes side by side.
    y = -np.log10(top['p'].clip(lower=1e-50))     # P below 1e-50 is drawn at 50.
    colour = np.where(top['chrom'] % 2 == 0, 'grey', 'steelblue')
    ax.scatter(x, y, c=colour, s=4)
    ax.axhline(-np.log10(5e-8), color='red')      # Genome-wide significance.
    ax.set_xticks(range(1, 23))
    ax.set_ylabel('-log10 P')


fig, axes = plt.subplots(3, 1, figsize=(10, 7), sharex=True)
for ax, trait in zip(axes, snps):
    manhattan(ax, snps[trait])
    ax.set_title(trait)
axes[-1].set_xlabel('Chromosome')
fig.suptitle('Figure 1. The strongest 1% of SNPs; red line: P = 5e-8')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Answer. Bivariate LDSC multiplies the z scores of two traits, so a SNP reported for opposite alleles would add to the sum with the wrong sign. Between 30% and 47% of the SNPs in each file were flipped. Lifting TG from GRCh38 to GRCh37 raised its matched SNPs from 9,849 to 988,678. Allele C of rs1421085 raises BMI (z = 45.76) and TG (z = 5.06) but lowers BMI-adjusted DBP (z = −3.65); see Q5. In Figure 1, BMI and DBP have genome-wide significant SNPs on every chromosome, while TG has a few strong regions.

Q4. Heritability¶

LDSC regresses each SNP's χ² on its LD score $\ell_j$:

$$E[\chi^2_j] = 1 + Na + \frac{N h^2}{M}\,\ell_j,$$

where $N$ is the sample size and $M$ the number of SNPs. The slope gives the heritability $h^2$, and the intercept $1 + Na$ measures confounding (Table 4, Figure 2).

Which trait is most heritable? Is there much confounding? Set M = ref.m_snps and rerun: what changes?

Answer. BMI 0.214 (SE 0.008), TG 0.178 (SE 0.022) and DBP 0.137 (SE 0.007). λGC is up to 2.7, but the intercepts are close to one (BMI 1.159, DBP 1.211), so most of the inflation is polygenic signal. TG's intercept, 0.960, is below one because the TG studies were corrected for genomic control. With M = ref.m_snps, every $h^2$ is 1.68 times larger: M decides which SNPs $h^2$ refers to.

Q5. Genetic correlations¶

Bivariate LDSC regresses the product of two traits' z scores on LD score. The slope, scaled by the two heritabilities, gives the genetic correlation $r_g$, and the intercept reflects people who are in both GWAS (Table 5, Figure 3).

Which traits are genetically correlated? UK Biobank is in both the BMI and the DBP GWAS, and DBP was adjusted for BMI. Can you explain the BMI–DBP result?

Answer. BMI–TG gives $r_g$ = 0.283 (SE 0.030) and TG–DBP 0.095 (SE 0.027). BMI–DBP is negative, −0.059 (SE 0.017), although higher BMI goes with higher blood pressure. Adjusting DBP for BMI subtracts part of each SNP's BMI effect, which pushes the genetic correlation with BMI down. This is collider bias (Aschard et al. 2015). The BMI–TG intercept, 0.051, reflects people in both GWAS; the BMI–DBP intercept is close to zero because BMI-adjusted DBP is uncorrelated with BMI.

Q6. Conclude¶

Write three sentences: the BMI–TG $r_g$ with its standard error, why it does not show that BMI raises TG, and what the BMI–DBP result teaches you about published GWAS.

Answer. BMI and TG have a genetic correlation of 0.28 (SE 0.03). It could come from BMI affecting TG, the reverse, pleiotropy or a shared cause, and MR or other designs are needed to tell these apart. Before using a published GWAS, check what it was adjusted for.

Data and methods¶

All three GWAS are European-ancestry meta-analyses from the GWAS Catalog (GCST006900, GCST002216, GCST006630; see data/sources.json), downloaded with allelign. The LD scores are the 1000 Genomes European scores released with LDSC (Zenodo 7768714); PLDSC leaves out about 30,000 SNPs in long-range LD regions such as the MHC, and SNPs with χ² above max(80, N/1000). Background: Bulik-Sullivan et al. 2015a and 2015b.
