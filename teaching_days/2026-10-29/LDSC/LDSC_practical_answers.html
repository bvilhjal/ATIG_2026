Answer. Chromosome and position were not found for TG and DBP, and allele frequency was found only for DBP. TG is matched by rsID instead, and we take the DBP positions from MarkerName. The frequency columns of BMI and TG come from other samples (HRS, 1000 Genomes), so it is just as well that they are not used.

The LDSC slope is $Nh^2/M$, so $h^2$ is estimated as slope × $M/N$: an N that is 10% too large gives an $h^2$ about 10% too small. N_effective discounts poorly imputed SNPs. Its median is 742,239 against 756,595 for TotalSampleSize. The HapMap3 SNPs are well imputed (at least 61% of the total N, median 99%), so here the choice matters little: with TotalSampleSize, DBP's h² is 0.136 instead of 0.137.

Q3. Match to the LD reference¶

LDSC needs an LD score for every SNP. We use scores for about 1.2 million HapMap3 SNPs, calculated from 489 Europeans in the 1000 Genomes Project; they come with PLDSC. PLDSC leaves out about 30,000 of them that lie in regions with unusually long-range LD, such as the MHC. allelign first removes duplicates and variants with low sample size. It then aligns each GWAS to the reference allele a1, changing the sign when a file's effect allele is a2 (Table 5).

Why is only a small part of the DBP file used? Table 6 follows rs1421085 in FTO through the three files. Why must all traits refer to the same allele before we correlate them?

Q4. Estimate SNP heritability¶

LDSC models the expected χ² statistic of SNP $j$ as

$$E[\chi^2_j] = 1 + Na + \frac{N h^2}{M}\,\ell_j, \qquad (1)$$

where $\ell_j$ is its LD score, $N$ the sample size, $M$ the number of SNPs the heritability $h^2$ refers to, and $a$ the inflation from confounding, such as population stratification. Figure 1 shows the mean χ² against LD score; Table 7 has the fits.

Which trait has the highest heritability? Compare each intercept with λGC, the median χ² divided by 0.455 (the median expected without any association). Which tells you more about confounding? Why might TG's intercept be below one? Then set M = ref.m_snps and rerun both cells. What changes, and what does not?

Answer. With the common-SNP M, BMI has the highest heritability (0.214, SE 0.008), followed by TG (0.176, SE 0.022) and DBP (0.137, SE 0.007). In Figure 1, mean χ² rises almost linearly with LD score, as equation (1) predicts. In the last few bins the points fall below the line for all three traits; the regression weights give SNPs with high LD scores little weight, so the fit follows the bulk of the SNPs. TG is noisier because its N is about eight times smaller.

λGC is 2.7 for BMI, but the intercept is only 1.159. In 690,000 people a polygenic trait inflates χ² everywhere, and λGC cannot separate that from confounding. The intercept can: about 6% of the excess mean χ² for BMI, (1.159 − 1)/(3.817 − 1), and 10% for DBP, is in the intercept. That is an upper bound on confounding, because the intercept also absorbs mismatch between the model or the LD reference and the data. Note also that the UK Biobank part of DBP had already been divided by its own LDSC intercept.

TG's intercept is 0.955 (SE 0.012), below one. GLGC corrected each study for genomic control, which divides all χ² by λGC and lowers both the intercept and the slope, so TG's $h^2$ is underestimated. The P-values in the file were corrected a second time: their χ² are about 11% smaller than those from beta/SE, which we use.

TG's $h^2$ also depends on a few loci with very large effects, which equation (1) does not describe well. The fit drops SNPs with χ² above max(80, N/1000), which is 342 SNPs for TG. Without that filter (chi2_cap=False), TG's $h^2$ is 0.29. The original LDSC software handles these SNPs with a two-step estimator instead.

With M = ref.m_snps (9,997,231 variants), every $h^2$ is 1.68 times larger: 0.36, 0.30 and 0.23. The intercepts, mean χ² and λGC do not change, and neither would $r_g$. M decides what $h^2$ refers to: the common SNPs (MAF 5–50%) of the reference panel, or all 10 million variants, assuming rare variants explain as much per standardised variant as common ones. Published LDSC estimates normally use the common-SNP M.

Q5. Genetic correlation between BMI and TG¶

Bivariate LDSC uses the product of the two traits' z scores:

$$E[z_{1j} z_{2j}] = \frac{\sqrt{N_1 N_2}\,\rho_g}{M}\,\ell_j + \frac{N_s\,\rho}{\sqrt{N_1 N_2}}, \qquad (2)$$

where $\rho_g$ is the genetic covariance, $N_s$ the number of people in both GWAS and $\rho$ the phenotypic correlation among them. The genetic correlation is $r_g = \rho_g / \sqrt{h^2_1 h^2_2}$.

Is the BMI–TG genetic correlation clearly different from zero? What does the cross-trait intercept tell you? The plain correlation of z scores across SNPs is much smaller than $r_g$. Why?

Answer. $r_g$ = 0.282 (SE 0.030), more than nine standard errors from zero. Variants that raise BMI tend to raise triglycerides.

The cross-trait intercept is 0.051 (SE 0.011). From equation (2), it reflects people in both GWAS (many cohorts contributed to both GIANT and GLGC) or confounding shared by the two. If the phenotypic correlation is around 0.3, it corresponds to roughly 0.051 × √(688,566 × 90,996) / 0.3 ≈ 43,000 shared participants. Because the intercept is estimated, the overlap does not bias $r_g$.

The plain correlation of z scores is 0.126. It is diluted by sampling noise: TG's mean χ² is only 1.21, so most of the variance of its z scores is noise. Averaging equation (2) over SNPs gives roughly [0.282 × √((3.817 − 1.159)(1.212 − 0.955)) + 0.051] / √(3.817 × 1.212) ≈ 0.13, close to what we see. LDSC uses only the part of $z_1 z_2$ that grows with LD score, which is genetic.

Answer. Higher BMI goes with higher blood pressure, so for unadjusted DBP a positive $r_g$ would be expected. Here BMI–DBP $r_g$ is −0.059 (SE 0.017). Adjusted for BMI, each SNP's DBP estimate is roughly its DBP effect minus $b$ times its BMI effect, where $b$ is the observational regression of DBP on BMI. Subtracting a multiple of the BMI genetic effects pushes the genetic covariance with BMI down, here to slightly below zero. This is collider bias from adjusting for a heritable covariate (Aschard et al. 2015); Evangelou et al. mention the risk. Table 6 showed this at one SNP: the BMI-raising allele of rs1421085 lowers BMI-adjusted DBP (z = −3.65).

The cross-trait intercept is $N_s\rho/\sqrt{N_1 N_2}$. Within UK Biobank, DBP adjusted for BMI is uncorrelated with BMI by construction, so $\rho \approx 0$ and the large overlap leaves no trace: 0.003 (SE 0.014). TG–DBP gives $r_g$ = 0.095 (SE 0.027).

Q7. Conclude¶

Write three sentences: report the BMI–TG genetic correlation with its standard error, explain why it does not show that higher BMI raises triglycerides, and say what the BMI–DBP result teaches you about using published GWAS results.

Answer. The genetic correlation between BMI and triglycerides is 0.28 (SE 0.03), estimated from about one million HapMap3 SNPs. It does not tell whether BMI raises triglycerides, triglyceride-related biology affects BMI, the same variants affect both through separate pathways, or both traits depend on something else; MR or other designs are needed for that. The BMI–DBP estimate shows that a covariate in a published GWAS changes what is estimated, so read the methods before combining summary statistics.

Data and method notes¶

The GWAS files are downloaded and checked against the SHA-256 checksums in the setup cell. All three are meta-analyses of European-ancestry samples with GRCh37 positions. BMI (GIANT) and DBP (discovery stage) come from the GWAS Catalog (GCST006900, GCST006630), TG from the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium (joint GWAS and Metabochip analysis). The TG studies were corrected for genomic control, and the meta-analysis P values were corrected again. For DBP, the UK Biobank statistics were divided by their LDSC intercept (1.219) and the ICBP studies corrected for genomic control before the meta-analysis. Further details are in data/sources.json.

The LD scores are the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European scores for HapMap3 SNPs released with LDSC (Zenodo 7768714), packaged in PLDSC as hm3-eur. The set has almost no strand-ambiguous A/T or C/G SNPs (4 of 1.19 million), and allelign drops those. PLDSC fits equations (1) and (2) in one step, after dropping SNPs with χ² above max(80, N/1000), and uses these LD scores for the regression weights; standard errors come from a block jackknife. PLDSC leaves out allelign's GRCh37 long-range LD regions by default (load_ld_reference('hm3-eur', include_long_range_ld=True) keeps them). The original LDSC software instead uses a two-step estimator, and its regression weights leave out only the MHC. Its estimates differ somewhat from these, most for TG, whose h² depends on how SNPs with very large χ² are handled. The plain z-score correlation in Q5 is shown only for contrast.

Background: Bulik-Sullivan et al. (2015a) on LDSC and Bulik-Sullivan et al. (2015b) on genetic correlation.
